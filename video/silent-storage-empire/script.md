## SCRIPT

### [0:00] COLD OPEN — The nephew and the 10x10
Your nephew Marcus called on a Sunday. He was moving out of his girlfriend's place, he had a truck full of furniture, and he needed somewhere to put it. Fast.

You drove over and helped him carry a couch into a storage unit off Route 9. Ten by ten. Orange door. Eighty-nine dollars a month.

The manager at the front desk, a kid named Tyler, slid Marcus a contract and a padlock and told him, "First month's half off. Owner's pretty generous."

Marcus laughed. "Must be nice to own one of these."

You didn't say anything.

You own that one. And two hundred and eleven others. In three countries.

The company Tyler works for is one small piece of a holding company worth about four hundred and eighty million dollars. Your name isn't on any of it. Not the sign. Not the website. Not the contract Marcus just signed.

And the reason nobody knows is the same reason you're still standing when the loudest guy in the business isn't.

It started with a gravel lot and a woman who kept everything in pencil.

### [0:50] CHAPTER 1 — One gravel lot and $41,000
You were twenty-four, working nights at a distribution warehouse, and you'd saved forty-one thousand dollars by living in a basement apartment with a space heater.

Everyone you knew wanted to buy a rental house. You drove past one on the way to work every night that nobody wanted: a self-storage lot with sixty-two units, a chain-link fence, and a hand-painted sign that said "SPACE AVAILABLE." Half the doors were rusted shut.

The owner was an eighty-year-old named Walt who wanted out. He was asking six hundred thousand. You had forty-one.

So you did the only thing that made sense. You asked who did Walt's books.

### [2:05] CHAPTER 2 — Ruth's notebook
Her name was Ruth. Seventy-three years old, retired bookkeeper, still doing taxes for half the small businesses in the county out of her kitchen.

She opened a green spiral notebook, flipped to a page labeled WALT – STORAGE, and turned it around so you could read it.

"Here's what nobody tells you about storage," she said. "No toilets. No tenants calling at midnight. No kitchens to remodel. People put things in a box, and then they forget about the box. And they keep paying."

Then she tapped the page. "Walt's charging forty-five dollars for a ten by ten. The place across town charges a hundred and five. He hasn't raised rent in nine years because he likes being liked."

That was the moment you understood you weren't buying a lot. You were buying a price nobody had touched.

Before you left, Ruth gave you three rules. You wrote them on the back of a receipt and kept that receipt in your wallet for twenty years.

**Rule one:** Never put your name on the sign. People will pay a company. They'll negotiate with a man.

**Rule two:** Never borrow money that can change its mind. Fixed rate, long term, or don't do the deal.

**Rule three:** Buy from people who are tired, never from people who are desperate. Tired people want a fair buyer. Desperate people want a miracle, and miracles come with lawyers.

### [3:30] CHAPTER 3 — The rent nobody had raised
You put down your forty-one thousand. A local bank lent you four hundred and twenty thousand, fixed for twenty-five years, against the lot itself. Walt carried the last hundred and forty thousand as a seller note, paid to him monthly.

"He'll be your lender," Ruth said, "and the tenants will be paying him."

The first thing you did wasn't raise rent. It was fix the doors. Then you put in a keypad gate and real lights. Then — and only for new customers — you charged seventy-five.

The units filled in four months.

Existing tenants went up five dollars a quarter until they reached market. Almost nobody left. It turns out people will pay a lot of money to never have to think about their stuff again.

In year one, Walt's lot made thirty-eight thousand dollars in profit. In your second year it made a hundred and forty-one thousand.

Same fence. Same gravel. Same sixty-two doors.

The company was called Ridgeline Storage. Named after the road. Not after you.

### [4:50] CHAPTER 4 — The Storage Baron
Now, there's someone you need to meet. Remember his face, because in a few years you'll be buying his buildings.

His name was Grant Lyle, but on billboards across the state he was "The Storage Baron." Top hat. Cartoon castle. A jingle on the radio every morning.

Grant didn't buy old lots. He built new ones. Glass fronts, climate control, four stories tall. And he paid for them with floating-rate construction loans at three percent, because three percent felt like it would last forever.

At a trade conference, Grant found you at the coffee table, looked at your name badge, and laughed.

"Ridgeline. You're the gravel guy." He told you that you were thinking too small. That money was cheap. That the people who won this decade would be the ones who built the fastest.

By then you owned nine facilities. He owned thirty-one.

You smiled, thanked him, and wrote down one more line on the back of Ruth's receipt:
**Cheap money is a loan from the future. The future always collects.**

### [6:00] CHAPTER 5 — When rates doubled
You were thirty-four when it happened.

Interest rates didn't go up. They jumped. Within eighteen months, the floating loans that had cost Grant three percent cost him over eight.

His buildings were beautiful and half-empty, because brand-new facilities take years to fill. His interest payments tripled. His lenders stopped extending. And then one bank took four facilities back, and the rest followed like dominoes.

Twenty-two months after he laughed at you, the Storage Baron's castle logo was spray-painted over by the people who bought his buildings at auction.

You know who bought six of them?

Your rate hadn't moved. Ruth's second rule had been sitting quietly in every loan agreement for ten years. Your old gravel lots were full, your debt was fixed, and when everyone else was desperate, you were just a little bit bored.

That's the best time to buy anything.

### [7:10] CHAPTER 6 — Buying at 8, selling at 5
This next part is where the real money is, so slow down with it.

Storage facilities are valued by something called a cap rate. If a facility earns a hundred thousand dollars a year and sells at an eight percent cap rate, it's worth one point two five million.

Small, single facilities owned by retiring families trade at seven or eight percent. Big professionally managed portfolios — the kind pension funds and large investors want to own — trade closer to five.

Same dollar of profit. Very different price.

At eight percent, a hundred thousand dollars of profit is worth one point two five million. At five, it's worth two million.

So every time you bought a tired family's lot and folded it into Ridgeline — same gate system, same pricing software, same cleaning schedule, same reporting — that lot became worth sixty percent more overnight. Not because you worked harder. Because of who it was now standing next to.

You never advertised that you were buying. You sent hand-written letters. You called Ruth's clients, then their friends, then their friends' accountants. And sellers kept hearing the same thing about you: "He fixes the doors and he keeps the manager."

You kept every manager. You kept every local name. Hollow Creek Storage stayed Hollow Creek Storage. Nobody driving past could see the company above all of them.

### [8:30] CHAPTER 7 — Going overseas without moving
At forty-one, a large investment firm offered you three hundred and ten million for everything.

You called Ruth first. She was ninety now, still doing taxes.

"What do they want it for?" she asked.

"To hold it for ten years and sell it to someone bigger."

She was quiet for a second. "Then they've already done the math on you," she said. "Make them do it again in ten years."

So you didn't sell the company. You sold a forty percent stake for a hundred and twenty-four million, kept control, and used every dollar to keep buying.

Tired owners with no plan for succession aren't just an American thing. Canada had them. So did the United Kingdom. You didn't move. You hired one quiet operator in each country, handed them the same playbook and Ruth's three rules printed on the first page, and gave them the capital.

By forty-six, Ridgeline owned two hundred and twelve facilities. Last year's appraisal put the whole company at four hundred and eighty million dollars.

### [9:40] CHAPTER 8 — The voicemail
Ruth died the spring you turned forty-five. She left you the green notebook.

The page that said WALT – STORAGE still had a pencil note in the corner you'd never noticed: "Good kid. Too quiet. That'll help him."

Last month a journalist called, writing a list of the richest people in the state nobody had ever heard of. You let it go to voicemail.

Then you drove to Route 9 and helped your nephew carry a couch.

### [10:30] CLOSE — The three rules, one more time
Here's what a gravel lot taught you.

**Never put your name on the sign.** Visibility is a cost you pay forever.

**Never borrow money that can change its mind.** The loudest people in any industry are usually standing on floating-rate debt.

**Buy from people who are tired, not people who are desperate.** And when the market makes everyone desperate, be the one who's simply bored and ready.

Somewhere off Route 9, there's a guy in an old sweatshirt helping his nephew with a couch. The manager thinks he's just a nice uncle.

That's exactly how he planned it.
