## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN
It's 5:40 on a Saturday morning, and you're on your knees behind a row of dryers with a flashlight in your teeth.

The floor is cold. The vent you're cleaning is packed with a gray felt of lint as thick as a winter glove. Your sweatshirt is eleven years old. There's a hole in the cuff you keep meaning to sew.

Behind you, the front door chimes. A college kid drags in a garbage bag full of clothes, looks around, and spots you on the floor.

"Hey, do you work here? The change machine ate my five."

You stand up, wipe your hands on your jeans, open the machine with a key from your ring, and hand him five singles.

"Yeah," you say. "I work here."

He says thanks and turns away. He'll never think about you again.

Your cousin Tyson will be awake in about four hours. He'll post a photo from the driver's seat of a black German sedan he leases for nine hundred and forty dollars a month. Last week he posted from a rooftop bar. The caption said, "Built different."

Tyson thinks you fix washing machines for a living. At family dinners, he slides you the check and says, "I got it, cuz. You're saving up, right?"

Here's what Tyson doesn't know.

The laundromat you're standing in is yours. So are thirty-seven others, across four cities. So are nineteen of the buildings they sit inside.

Last year, your company did a little over eleven million dollars in revenue. Your share of what it's worth, on a conservative day, is about twenty-three million.

Tyson's credit card balance is somewhere north of thirty-one thousand. He told you once, after a few drinks, and then never mentioned it again.

You look poorer than you are. Tyson looks richer than he is. And nobody at that family dinner table knows either thing.

You didn't plan any of this. Seventeen years ago, you couldn't afford to do your own laundry.

This is the story of how that changed, and what it cost you along the way.

### LEVEL ONE — The jar of quarters
You're twenty-four. You fix commercial washers and dryers for a route company that services apartment buildings. Twenty-one dollars an hour. A van that smells like detergent and burnt belts.

Your apartment doesn't have a washer. So every Sunday night, you carry a duffel bag four blocks to a place called Sunny Wash.

Sunny Wash is tired. Half the dryers have handwritten "OUT OF ORDER" signs. The fluorescent lights buzz. The floor has a crack shaped like a lightning bolt.

The owner is a woman named Dolores. Sixty-eight. Reading glasses on a beaded chain. She sits behind the counter with a crossword and a coffee can full of quarters.

One night, dryer number six stops mid-cycle with your clothes still wet. You don't complain. You pull the back panel off with the screwdriver on your keychain, find a snapped belt, and tell her the part number.

Dolores puts down her crossword.

"You know machines," she says.

"It's my job."

"Then fix the other four," she says, "and you'll never pay for a wash in here again."

So you do. It takes three Sundays.

On the third one, she pours you coffee from a thermos and turns her ledger around so you can see it. Real numbers, written in blue pen.

"People think this is a coin business," she says. "It's not. It's a water and electricity business. Every load costs me about forty cents to run. I charge three dollars and twenty-five cents. Nobody negotiates. Nobody sends an invoice thirty days late. They pay before the machine even starts."

You stare at the page. On a good month, a broken-down place like Sunny Wash still clears about eight thousand dollars after every bill.

"Why don't you fix it up?" you ask.

"I'm tired," she says. "My husband ran this. He's been gone four years. I keep it open because closing it feels like losing him twice."

That night, you go home and do something you've never done. You open a separate savings account and name it "Machines."

You set up an automatic transfer. Two hundred dollars, every payday.

It isn't exciting. It isn't supposed to be. It's just money you've decided you will never see.

### LEVEL TWO — The laundromat nobody wanted
Two years later, Dolores calls you on a Tuesday.

"I'm selling," she says. "A guy from a big chain came by. He wants the corner for a phone store. He'd close the laundromat the day he got the keys."

"How much is he offering?"

"Three hundred and ten thousand."

You do the math in your head. The business clears about ninety-six thousand dollars a year. Small laundromats like hers usually sell for three to four times what they clear. So the price isn't crazy. It's actually fair.

The problem is you have forty-four thousand dollars in your "Machines" account.

You almost say no. Then you remember something your boss said once, on a service call at a retirement community. "Nobody pays cash for a business. The business pays for itself."

So you learn how small business loans actually work. You read everything. You sit with a loan officer named Gary at a community bank, in a tie you bought that morning.

Here's the deal you build.

You put down thirty-one thousand dollars, ten percent. The bank lends two hundred and thirty-two thousand, backed by a government small business program and paid over ten years. Dolores carries the last forty-seven thousand herself, as a seller note. You pay her every month, with interest, out of the laundromat's own cash.

"Why would I take payments when the chain is offering cash?" Dolores asks.

"Because the chain will tear it down," you say. "I'll keep the name. And I'll keep Rosa."

Rosa is the attendant who's folded laundry at Sunny Wash for fourteen years.

Dolores is quiet for a long time. Then she signs.

That's the first thing you learn about buying businesses. Price matters. But for a lot of owners, what happens to the place after they leave matters more.

You keep thirteen thousand dollars as a cushion, because Dolores gave you one rule before handing over the keys.

"Never buy a business with your last dollar," she says. "Machines break on the worst possible day."

She's right. Your first week as an owner, the main water heater dies. Two thousand nine hundred dollars, installed. If you'd spent your last dollar on the down payment, Sunny Wash would have closed in its first week under your name.

Your first full month, you write every number into a notebook. Water and sewer, two thousand one hundred dollars. Gas for the dryers, one thousand eight hundred. Electricity, one thousand three hundred. Rosa's pay, insurance, the bank loan, Dolores's note, repairs.

Revenue that month is twenty-two thousand four hundred dollars. After every single bill, you keep six thousand two hundred.

It's the first time in your life money has come in while you were asleep.

### LEVEL THREE — The years nothing happened
Nothing dramatic happens for a long time.

You keep your day job for the first eighteen months. Nights and weekends, you're at Sunny Wash.

You fix every machine. You replace the buzzing lights with bright LEDs. You patch the lightning bolt crack in the floor. You put in card readers so people don't need quarters, and the average customer starts spending more, because a card doesn't run out the way a pocketful of coins does.

Then you add something Dolores never had: wash and fold. People drop off a bag in the morning and pick it up folded by evening. You charge by the pound. Rosa runs it, and you give her a raise and a share of every pound.

In year one, Sunny Wash clears ninety-six thousand dollars. In year three, it clears one hundred and sixty-one thousand.

Same building. Same corner. Same lightning bolt in the floor, just filled in.

Nobody sees this happen. There's no moment to announce.

That same year, Tyson leases his first luxury car. Six hundred and eighty dollars a month. He picks you up from a family barbecue and taps the dashboard screen.

"You should get one of these," he says. "Life's short. You can afford it, can't you?"

"Probably," you say.

You mean it. You could afford it.

But by now you've done a different kind of math. That six hundred and eighty a month, sent to a laundromat instead of a lease, could buy a used commercial washer every two months. And a commercial washer, in a good location, earns back its own price in about two years and keeps earning for fifteen.

A car loses value the day you drive it home. A washer pays you every single time somebody does a load.

You don't explain any of this to Tyson. He wouldn't want to hear it. And honestly, it isn't your business how he spends his money.

At twenty-nine, you finally quit the route company. Your boss shakes your hand and says, "Call me if it doesn't work out."

You never call. But you do send him a card every Christmas, because he taught you the one thing that made all of this possible: how to fix a machine instead of replacing it.

By then, you've paid Dolores every cent of her note, on time, every month. On the last payment, she mails you back a postcard. On the front is a picture of a beach. On the back she's written, "Told you machines pay."

### LEVEL FOUR — The bad year
You're thirty-one when the chain comes back.

They don't buy you out. They open a brand-new location three blocks away. It's called SpinCity. Glass front. Fifty machines. A phone app. A grand opening with a DJ and free detergent for a month.

In the first sixty days, your revenue drops twenty-eight percent.

Your bank payment doesn't drop. Your payment to Dolores doesn't drop. Rosa's paycheck doesn't drop.

Every instinct you have says cut prices. Match them. Fight.

You call Dolores. She's seventy-five now, and still does the crossword in pen.

"Don't race them to the bottom," she says. "They have more money than you. You'll lose that race. Find the customers they can't be bothered with."

So you do something boring. You drive around and knock on doors.

A gym with three hundred towels a day. Two vacation rental hosts who turn over sheets every weekend. A small nursing home with a broken laundry room. A barbershop. A yoga studio.

You offer pickup, wash, fold, and delivery. Commercial accounts, monthly invoices, simple contracts.

Here's why that matters. Walk-in customers come and go. A commercial account with a nursing home comes every single day, whether the economy is good or bad, whether there's a shiny new laundromat down the street or not.

Within a year, commercial accounts are thirty-four percent of your revenue. Sunny Wash is clearing more than it ever did before SpinCity opened.

Eighteen months later, SpinCity's parent company, buried in debt from opening too many stores too fast, closes four locations. Including the one three blocks away.

You buy its fifty machines at auction for nineteen cents on the dollar.

You write a new line in the notebook you've started keeping, the way Dolores kept her ledger.

"Don't fight the loud competitor. Serve the customers they ignore."

### LEVEL FIVE — The quiet roll-up
By thirty-four, Sunny Wash, its second location, and its commercial route are clearing about four hundred thousand dollars a year.

And you've noticed something.

Laundromat owners are old. Most of them bought their stores in the nineteen-eighties and nineties. Their kids don't want them. Like Dolores, they're tired.

They don't trust big chains. They've seen what chains do to neighborhoods. They want to sell to someone who will keep the name, keep the staff, and keep the doors open.

Someone like you.

You form a holding company. You name it Northgate Laundry Group, after the street your first apartment was on. It doesn't appear on a single sign.

Every store keeps its old name. Sunny Wash. Bubbles on Fifth. The Spin Cycle. Corner Coin Wash. Customers never notice anything changed except that the machines suddenly all work.

There's a second reason you buy quietly, and it's the part most people miss.

A single neighborhood laundromat, owned by one tired person, usually sells for three or four times what it clears in a year. But a group of twenty laundromats, with managers, systems, and clean books, sells for something closer to six or seven times.

Same washers. Same quarters. Same dryer lint.

So every time you buy a tired owner's store for three and a half times its profit and fold it into Northgate, that store becomes worth almost twice as much, simply because of the company it now belongs to. You never had to make a single extra dollar for that to happen.

And you follow one new rule, the most important one you'll ever make.

Whenever you can, buy the building too.

Here's why. When you only own the laundromat, you pay rent to a landlord, and the landlord can raise it, or refuse to renew your lease, or sell to a developer.

When you own the building, the laundromat pays rent to another company you own. The rent stays in the family. And over time, the building itself gets more valuable, while the loan on it gets smaller.

You do it the same way every time. Ten to twenty percent down. A bank loan backed by the store's cash flow. The seller carries a note when they're willing.

Store four. Store seven. Store twelve.

Not every deal works.

Store nine is a beautiful location near a college campus, and the owner won't sell the building, only the business. You buy it anyway. It's a great store. For three years it clears more than any other location you own.

Then the lease comes up for renewal. The landlord has watched your lines out the door every Sunday. He triples the rent.

You run the numbers twenty different ways. None of them work. You move all thirty-two machines to a smaller spot two blocks over, and the whole thing costs you one hundred and forty thousand dollars and four months of revenue.

You write it in the notebook, underlined twice.

"If you don't own the dirt, you don't own the business. You're renting it."

From then on, you almost never buy a store without its building again.

By thirty-eight, Northgate owns twenty-two laundromats and eleven of the buildings. Every store gets the same playbook within ninety days: card readers, LED lighting, wash and fold, commercial delivery, and a manager who owns a small share of their store's profit.

Rosa, who's been with you since Sunny Wash, runs operations for all of them.

Tyson, meanwhile, is on his third leased car. He's started a sneaker reselling business. He asks if you want to invest. "Ten grand and I'll double it in six months, guaranteed."

You tell him you'll think about it.

You don't, because nothing that's guaranteed to double in six months ever is.

Two years later, the sneaker market crashes. The bank repossesses Tyson's car from the parking lot of his apartment at six in the morning. He calls you that afternoon, embarrassed, asking if you can lend him four thousand dollars until things turn around.

You don't lend him the money.

Instead, you offer him a job driving one of the commercial delivery vans. Thirty-one dollars an hour, benefits, and a share of every new account he signs.

He's quiet for a long time. Then he says yes.

He's good at it. It turns out the same thing that made Tyson great at posting photos makes him great at walking into a gym and talking the owner into a laundry contract. Within a year, he's signed nineteen new commercial accounts.

He still has no idea you own the company. As far as he knows, his boss is Rosa. He's leased another German sedan since. Some things don't change, and that's fine. It's his money.

### LEVEL SIX — The phone call you didn't take
You're forty-one when a private equity firm calls.

They've been buying up laundromat chains across the country, and they've done the math on you. They offer fifteen million dollars for everything.

It's more money than anyone in your family has ever seen.

You call Dolores. She's eighty-four now. She lives with her daughter and still does the crossword, though not in pen anymore.

"What do they want it for?" she asks.

"To grow it for five years, then sell it to someone even bigger."

She laughs, a little. "Honey, nobody buys a laundromat to sell it for less. If it's worth fifteen to them today, it's worth more to you tomorrow."

You don't sell.

Instead, you sell them something else. You sell a minority stake in the operating company for four point two million dollars, and you keep every single building.

You use part of the money to pay off the highest-interest loans. You use the rest to buy six more stores.

That's the quiet secret of the whole thing. The laundromats make the money. The buildings hold the money. And the buildings keep going up in value whether anybody's doing laundry on a Tuesday or not.

Today, Northgate owns thirty-eight laundromats and nineteen buildings. The business pays you a salary that would make Tyson's jaw drop. You still drive a fourteen-year-old pickup.

Dolores died last spring. At the funeral, her daughter handed you an envelope.

Inside was a page torn from that old blue-pen ledger, the one she turned around for you that Sunday night. In the margin, in shaky handwriting, she'd written one line.

"He fixed dryer six for free. Sell it to him."

### CLOSE
Which brings you back to 5:40 on a Saturday morning, on your knees behind a row of dryers.

You don't have to clean the vents yourself anymore. You have people for that. Good people who get paid well.

But once a month, you come in early and do it anyway. It reminds you what the business actually is. Water, electricity, machines that work, and people who show up.

Tonight, Tyson will slide you the check at dinner again. "I got it, cuz."

You'll let him. It makes him happy.

And here's what a broken dryer taught you.

Buy the boring business people walk past every day. Don't race the loud competitor; serve the customers they ignore. And whenever you can, own the building, not just the business.

Somewhere on a quiet corner, there's a guy in an old sweatshirt handing a college kid five singles from the change machine. The kid thinks he's just the maintenance guy.

That's exactly how he planned it.
